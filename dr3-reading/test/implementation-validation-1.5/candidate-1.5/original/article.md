For years, aligning marketing and sales has been one of the most persistent priorities in commercial leadership—and one of the hardest to achieve. Agentic AI is changing that. Most organizations, however, are still approaching AI transformation function by function, embedding it separately within marketing and within sales rather than across the system that connects them. The starting point is a simple but often overlooked truth: Customers do not distinguish between the two. Every touchpoint, regardless of which function owns it internally, is experienced as a part of a single relationship. The organizations that design around that reality, rather than around their own organizational charts, are building a closed-loop go-to-market model where the two functions effectively operate as one. That’s our advice to companies, based on our more than 100 years of combined experience modernizing sales and marketing organizations of all sizes. Customers Expect Consistent Experiences Buying journeys no longer follow a linear path. Customers research, evaluate, and decide across an expanding set of touchpoints, often looping backward or pausing before moving forward. McKinsey research shows that more than 80% of consumers use multiple channels to research or purchase products, and B2B buyers now engage across ten interaction channels during a typical journey—double the number from less than a decade ago. These shifts raise the bar for execution. Relevance now depends on understanding intent across channels, maintaining context across interactions, and responding quickly enough to matter. When marketing and sales operate on separate data sets and disconnected systems, the customer journey is broken and experience suffers. A personalized marketing campaign may be followed by an AI-enabled sales outreach driven by different data, different assumptions, and different timing. Each function may be performing well against its own metrics while collectively delivering a fragmented experience. The cost of that fragmentation is measurable: McKinsey’s 2026 B2B Pulse survey finds that buyers cite inconsistent information across teams as the top reason for switching suppliers. Omnichannel execution has become the minimum required to compete. The differentiating factor is now integration. The same research finds that market leaders are four times more likely to deploy true one-to-one personalization and are further ahead in embedding AI into integrated commercial workflows. AI Makes the Case for Integration Agentic AI is accelerating a convergence already underway, with execution increasingly spanning what were once distinct marketing and sales activities. According to McKinsey’s The State of AI report, the most consistent revenue gains from AI are concentrated in marketing and sales. Among organizations using AI in these functions, 7% report revenue increases exceeding 10%. report revenue increases exceeding 10%. This convergence is most visible where customer segmentation and sales enablement align, outreach adapts as intent emerges, and sellers work alongside AI agents that maintain account context. Activities that once moved sequentially across teams increasingly operate in continuous loops, with signals interpreted in real time, actions coordinated across channels, and outcomes fed directly back into the system. As execution becomes continuous, accountability shifts as well. Marketing and sales increasingly share responsibility for pipeline quality, conversion, and customer value. The advantage compounds only when both functions contribute to and draw from the same data system. How Leading Organizations Are Adapting The performance effects are already visible both in our research and our work with companies. Cycle times compress as campaign creation and outreach development shrink from weeks to minutes. Conversion rates improve as interactions become more relevant to customer intent. Pipeline quality increases as engagement becomes more targeted and informed. Most importantly, customer experiences become more consistent because context carries across interactions instead of resetting at every stage. Consider a Fortune 500 tech company we worked with that was seeking to expand into a largely untouched segment of tens of thousands of commercial customers, with a small sales team that had limited capacity. Diagnostics revealed that sellers spent too much time working low-probability leads. An end-to-end AI solution enriched leads with various signals (for example, M&A activity), prioritized leads with the highest conversion likelihood, surfaced relevant account intelligence, and enabled agentic AI outreach. Within eight weeks, pipeline results were evident. AI-augmented sellers were sending five times the number of messages to leads while maintaining pre-AI open rates, response rates, and meeting scheduling rates. Further, AI-augmented seller prep time for initial calls dropped from 1–2 hours to 10–15 minutes. A leading global job search platform we’ve worked with offers another example on the sales side. The company expects its AI-enabled sales model to generate $30–$60 million in incremental annual revenue, contributing to a $400–$700 million uplift from agent-enabled use cases. The system deploys an AI sales development representative (SDR) that identifies prospects, generates personalized outreach, nurtures early conversations, and hands off qualified leads to sellers with complete context and next actions. What makes the model work is not the agent alone but the shared data and coordinated handoffs behind it. These results are reshaping how teams are structured. A new roles is emerging: the go-to-market engineer, who is increasingly responsible for designing and managing agentic workflows that span functions, align agent behavior with customer buying journeys, and ensure continuity across interactions. These capabilities barely existed a few years ago and are quickly becoming sources of competitive advantage. When Execution Converges, Ownership Sharpens The stakes are highest when AI agents interact directly with customers. When different functions build and deploy customer-facing agents independently, that coherence can erode. Marketing agents, sales agents, and service workflows may each optimize for local objectives while unintentionally fragmenting the broader customer narrative. Without shared standards and clear ownership, AI can scale inconsistency as easily as it scales efficiency. The AI SDR example at the job search platform illustrates this challenge. The agent continuously interprets intent, adapts outreach, and advances conversations until human involvement creates the most value. Traditionally, SDRs sit within sales. In an agentic model, however, the role spans both marketing and sales, combining demand generation, personalization, and early-stage engagement into a single, continuous workflow. Brand standards and customer context travel with the agent across every step. The Closed-Loop Model From what we’re seeing in our research and our work with companies, the organizations pulling ahead are redesigning marketing and sales as a single system. Rather than layering AI onto existing processes, they rethink the work itself, identifying where signals emerge, where decisions occur, and where coordination breaks down. Success requires two shifts. The first is technological: a shared customer data foundation and coordinated systems that allow marketers, sellers, and AI agents to operate from the same context. The second is organizational: shared incentives and metrics that measure outcomes across the full customer journey. Traditional distinctions of marketing-qualified leads versus sales-qualified leads give way to measures of pipeline quality, conversion, and customer lifetime value. When these elements come together, the customer experience changes fundamentally. Interactions become continuous rather than fragmented. Context carries forward instead of resetting at every stage. Humans and AI agents work together in a closed loop where segmentation evolves in real time, outreach is coordinated across channels, human judgment shapes the moments that matter most, and every insight feeds forward rather than resetting with each campaign or deal. For commercial leaders, the question is no longer whether this model is coming. It is whether their organization is building toward it or waiting until fragmentation becomes a competitive disadvantage. For leaders ready to pressure-test their own organization, the right questions span three areas: Vision and outcomes: Is your AI transformation for marketing and sales a shared one or two parallel efforts that happen to coexist? Are marketing and sales measured against a common definition of growth‒one that spans pipeline quality, conversion, and customer lifetime value? Data and systems: Do your marketing and sales operate from a shared, real-time view of the customer, or from parallel data sets that reflect internal boundaries more than customer reality? When agents or sellers engage a prospect, do they have the full context of every prior interaction? Do both functions share a roadmap for an integrated technology stack and data infrastructure? Organization and capabilities: Are you building the skills and the new commercial profiles that a coordinated agentic model requires? Are your marketers learning to direct AI systems that extend into sales territory and your sellers learning to engage customers already shaped by AI-driven interactions? . . . The technology to build a closed-loop go-to-market system is no longer the constraint. The advantage will belong to organizations whose leaders have the mandate and discipline to design around the customer rather than around the organizational chart.
